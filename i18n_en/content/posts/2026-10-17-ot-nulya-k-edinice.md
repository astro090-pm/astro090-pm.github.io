Key ideas from the PayPal co-founder's book on startups: why copying doesn't create the future, why aim for a monopoly, and which questions to ask yourself before starting.

Peter Thiel co-founded PayPal and Palantir and was Facebook's first outside investor. The book grew out of his Stanford class, notes from which were taken by Blake Masters, the co-author.

## Zero to one or one to n

Scaling what already works is going "from one to n". Creating something that didn't exist is going "from zero to one". Thiel argues that real progress comes only from the latter.

## The contrarian question

"What important truth do very few people agree with you on?" A good answer is the basis of a company that sees an opportunity others missed.

## Competition is for losers

Under intense competition, profits go to zero. The goal is to build a monopoly in your segment. Its features:

- **proprietary technology** at least 10 times better than alternatives;
- **network effects** — the product becomes more valuable with every user;
- **economies of scale**;
- **a strong brand**.

Start with a small market you can dominate completely, then expand.

## Sales matter as much as product

Many engineers believe a good product sells itself. Thiel insists distribution is part of the product, and without a well-thought-out channel even the best solution won't take off.

## Seven questions for a startup

Engineering, timing, monopoly, people, distribution, durability and the secret — the team needs a good answer to each.

## What a PM can take from it

Before launch, check: in what way is our product many times better, not a few percent; in which small market can we become first; and how exactly will the product reach users.

## Who should read it

Founders and PMs launching something new. The book is polemical — it's worth reading while arguing with the author.
