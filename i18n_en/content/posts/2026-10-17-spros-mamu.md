Key ideas from a short book on customer development: why users politely tell us what we want to hear, and how to ask questions nobody can lie about.

The title refers to a simple test: if you ask your mom whether your idea is good, she'll say yes because she loves you. Rob Fitzpatrick shows that almost every interviewee behaves the same way. The problem isn't people, it's the questions.

## Three rules of a good question

- **Talk about their life, not your idea.** Once you pitch the idea, the other person starts judging it — and sparing your feelings.
- **Ask about specifics in the past, not opinions about the future.** "When did this last happen? What did you do?" instead of "Would you use this?".
- **Talk less, listen more.** An interview isn't a pitch.

## Three kinds of bad data

- **Compliments** — "Great idea!" is worth nothing.
- **Fluff** — "I usually", "I would", "someday" are promises, not facts.
- **Ideas and feature requests** — behind a request, find the motivation: why the person needs it and what they do today.

## Commitments instead of promises

A good meeting ends with the other person risking something: time (a next meeting, a trial), reputation (an introduction to their boss) or money (a pre-order, a pilot). If all you get is "keep me posted", it's a polite no.

## How to prepare

- Write down the three big questions whose answers would change your decisions.
- Narrow the segment: while it's unclear whom to ask, answers will contradict each other.
- Keep the conversation casual and write down exact quotes, not your conclusions.

## What a PM can take from it

Before every customer interview, run your questions through the "mom test": can this be answered out of politeness? If so, rephrase it into a question about past behaviour.

## Who should read it

Anyone who interviews users — especially before their first round of customer development. It takes an evening to read and saves months of building things nobody needs.
