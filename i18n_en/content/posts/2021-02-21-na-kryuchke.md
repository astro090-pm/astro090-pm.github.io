Key ideas from the book about products people use out of habit, without ads or reminders: the four steps a habit is built from, and where the line with manipulation lies.

Nir Eyal has long worked at the intersection of psychology, technology and business. In the book he describes the Hook model — a cycle the user goes through again and again until the product becomes part of their day.

## The Hook model: four steps

1. **Trigger.** First external — a notification, an email, a button. Over time it's replaced by an internal one: boredom, anxiety, the urge to talk to someone.
2. **Action.** The simplest behaviour done in anticipation of a reward. It happens when motivation, ability and a trigger coincide, so every extra step lowers the chance of action.
3. **Variable reward.** A reward you can't predict exactly holds attention more strongly. Eyal names three kinds: the tribe (approval from others), the hunt (new information, finds) and the self (mastery, completion).
4. **Investment.** The user puts something into the product — data, content, followers, settings, reputation. This makes the product more valuable to them and loads the next trigger.

## When a habit is possible

A habit forms when a product is used often and is clearly useful. A rare but very useful behaviour can also become a habit, but it's harder.

## Ethics

Eyal proposes a matrix: would you use the product yourself, and does it materially improve people's lives? Yes and yes — you're a "facilitator". It doesn't improve lives but you use it — an "entertainer". It improves lives but you don't use it — a "peddler". Neither — a "dealer".

## What a PM can take from it

Break the key scenario into the four steps: which internal trigger we serve, how many steps to the action, what varies in the reward, and what the user leaves in the product so they come back.

## Who should read it

PMs of consumer products and anyone working on retention. Read it together with the ethics chapter — the model works equally well for helpful and harmful products.
