Thinking, Fast and Slow by Daniel Kahneman: how we get decisions wrong

29 August 2021booksmanagement

Key ideas from the Nobel laureate's book on the two systems of thinking and the systematic errors everyone makes — including in product decisions.

Daniel Kahneman was a psychologist and winner of the Nobel Prize in Economics for his work on decision-making under uncertainty. The book brings together decades of research he did with Amos Tversky.

Two systems

  • System 1 is fast, automatic and effortless: it recognises faces, completes phrases, produces first impressions.
  • System 2 is slow and effortful: it calculates, compares, checks. It's lazy and often just endorses what System 1 suggested.

Errors worth knowing

  • Anchoring. The first number you hear affects your estimate, even if it's random.
  • Availability. We judge as likely what comes to mind easily: vivid, recent, loud.
  • What you see is all there is. The mind builds a coherent story from the facts at hand and doesn't notice what's missing.
  • Planning fallacy. We estimate time and cost from the best-case scenario. The cure is the "outside view": compare with similar projects instead of only analysing your own.
  • Loss aversion. A loss feels stronger than an equal gain — the basis of prospect theory.
  • Overconfidence. An expert's confidence reflects the coherence of their story, not the accuracy of the forecast.

Two selves

The "experiencing self" lives in the moment; the "remembering self" judges an experience by its peak and its end. So the impression of a service depends most on its most intense moment and how it ended.

What a PM can take from it

  • Estimate timelines through similar projects, not just the plan.
  • Run a premortem before launch: imagine the project failed and write down why. Kahneman cites this technique from psychologist Gary Klein.
  • Remember the peak and the end in the user journey: the last screen of a scenario matters more than it seems.

Who should read it

Anyone who makes decisions based on data and other people's opinions. The book is long — it's easiest to read in parts, returning to the chapters on biases.
